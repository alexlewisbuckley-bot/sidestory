Sunday Service

Written by Morgan Childs · seven-minute read · the story that became a fragrance

The road home has not been resurfaced in twenty years, and neither has the part of me that drives it. Past the reservoir the trees close over the lane the way they always did, and there — still standing, still rusted the colour of a wet penny — is the tower we were forbidden to climb and climbed anyway, the summer we were fifteen.

The pond below it is smaller than memory allows. We swam in it until our lips went blue and lied to our mothers about where we had been. On the dock at the far end, on an evening in August, Ruth Kelleher kissed me and then laughed, which I have since learned is the correct order.

I am late for my niece’s christening. I park badly, as everyone does here, and cross the gravel with my collar wrong. Inside, the cool oaky air arrives before the organ does — polish and cold stone and the ghost of last week’s lilies — and I am eleven again in an itching jumper, and thirty-eight, and neither.

“The music was the same.
The cool, oaky air of the church was the same.”

My sister hands me the baby to hold during the second hymn and I hold her the way you hold something that has just been finished. The font is the one I was held over. The window above it is the one I counted panes in, badly, for a decade of Sundays.

In my right-hand pocket, all through the service, my thumb finds the ring box and turns it over, and over. I have carried it three weeks. I will ask her on the dock, I think. In August, if the weather holds.

— M.C.
